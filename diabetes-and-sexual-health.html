Diabetes and Sexual Health: Why It Happens and What You Can Do

This is a common problem that many people are too shy to ask about. It is not your fault, and it is often treatable.

Short answer: sexual problems are common in people with diabetes. A large review of 145 studies found erectile dysfunction in about half of men with diabetes.1 Women are affected too.2 The causes include high blood sugar damaging nerves and blood vessels, but also stress, low mood, weight, smoking and some medicines. Good blood sugar control helps. See a doctor, because it can also be an early warning sign for your heart. Do not buy unregulated 'sexual power' products.

How common is it?

More common than most people think. A systematic review of 145 studies, covering 88,577 men, found that about 52.5% of men with diabetes had erectile dysfunction, after adjusting for publication bias. Men with diabetes had roughly 3.6 times the odds of erectile dysfunction compared with men without diabetes.1

Women are affected too. A review of 26 studies compared 3,168 women with diabetes and 2,823 women without. It found that sexual problems were more common in women with diabetes.2

Estimates vary between studies. The Diabetes Canada guideline gives a range of about 34% to 45% of adult men with diabetes,4 and it advises that all adult men with diabetes be asked about erectile function regularly.4 So if your doctor has never asked, it is fine to bring it up yourself.

Many people never mention it to a doctor. If this is you, you are not alone.

Why diabetes affects sexual health

  • Nerve damage. High sugar over years can damage nerves, including those that control arousal and sensation. Nerve damage (neuropathy) is a well-known cause of erectile dysfunction in diabetes. See also diabetes complications and foot care, which relate to the same nerve risk.
  • Blood vessel damage. Erections and arousal depend on good blood flow. Diabetes, high blood pressure and high cholesterol can narrow vessels. See blood pressure and cholesterol.
  • Higher HbA1c, longer duration of diabetes, older age and smoking are all linked with erectile dysfunction. See HbA1c numbers.
  • Mind and mood. Worry, stress, poor sleep and low mood can reduce desire. They are common in people living with a long-term illness. See stress, sleep and blood sugar and fatigue.
  • Medicines and other conditions. Some medicines for blood pressure or mood can affect sexual function. Low testosterone, thyroid problems, and for women, menopause and vaginal dryness or infections, can also play a part. Ask your doctor before changing any medicine.

For men

Erectile dysfunction means trouble getting or keeping an erection firm enough for sex. In diabetes it often develops gradually.

It can be a heart warning. The blood vessels to the penis are small. Problems there can appear before problems in the heart vessels. That is why a doctor should check your blood pressure, cholesterol, sugar and heart health if you notice this. It is not only a bedroom problem.

Tablets called PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil and similar) are the main treatment for erectile dysfunction.4 They work less well in some men with diabetes who also have low testosterone, so a doctor may check your hormone levels.4 They are not safe for everyone:

  • Never take them with nitrates. The FDA-approved label for Viagra (sildenafil) lists nitrates, such as nitroglycerin used for chest pain, as a contraindication. The label says sildenafil increased the blood-pressure-lowering effect of nitrates.3 The label also says it is not known how long after sildenafil nitrates can be used safely.3
  • Tell your doctor about your other medicines. The label warns that sildenafil can add to the blood-pressure-lowering effect of alpha blockers and blood pressure tablets, which can cause low blood pressure.3
  • Your heart must be fit for sex. The label says not to use it if sexual activity is unsafe because of your heart condition.3
  • Know the emergency signs. Seek urgent medical help for an erection lasting more than 4 hours, for sudden loss of vision in one eye, or for sudden hearing loss.3

This is why you should take these tablets only after a doctor reviews your medicines and your heart. Do not borrow a friend's tablets or buy them from an unknown online seller.

For women

Women with diabetes may notice lower desire, vaginal dryness, pain during sex or difficulty with arousal or orgasm.2 Causes include nerve and blood vessel changes, repeated vaginal or urinary infections, hormonal changes and mood.

These problems are often ignored, but they are real and can be improved. The ADA added a section on female sexual dysfunction to its 2025 standards. It advises health professionals to ask about sexual health, especially in women with depression or anxiety and in those with repeated urinary infections.5 A gynaecologist or a doctor you trust can check for infections, hormone changes and medicine effects, and suggest treatment. Do not be embarrassed. Doctors see this often.

What to expect at the doctor

A good consultation is short and private. You can expect questions like:

  • When did the change start? Was it sudden or slow?
  • Do you still get morning erections (men)? Is there pain, dryness or loss of desire (women)?
  • Which medicines do you take, including herbal ones and supplements? Some blood pressure, mood and other medicines can affect sexual function.
  • How are your mood, sleep and stress? Do you smoke or drink?

The doctor may check blood pressure, HbA1c, cholesterol, kidney function and, for men, possibly testosterone. They may also examine your feet and nerves, because the same nerve damage can show up in both places. It helps to write your questions down. You can bring your tracking numbers (how to track).

If you feel too shy to talk face to face, say one sentence: "I have a sexual health problem I would like to discuss." Doctors hear this every day.

What helps

  1. See a doctor, and be specific. Say plainly what has changed. A diabetes doctor, physician, urologist or gynaecologist can all help. Ask for a check of sugar, blood pressure, cholesterol and heart risk.
  2. Improve blood sugar and weight. Better control protects nerves and vessels over the long term. Our 12-week plan and guide on weight loss show how.
  3. Stop smoking. Smoking is a known risk factor for erectile dysfunction.
  4. Limit alcohol. Heavy drinking can reduce sexual function. Read alcohol and diabetes.
  5. Move more and sleep well. See movement and sleep.
  6. Talk with your partner. Silence often adds worry. A simple conversation can reduce pressure.
  7. Avoid unregulated "sexual power" products. Ads promise quick fixes, but such products may be unsafe and some may contain hidden drugs. Use only what a doctor prescribes.
Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Does diabetes cause erectile dysfunction?

It is a common cause. A review of 145 studies found that about half of men with diabetes had erectile dysfunction. High blood sugar over years can damage nerves and blood vessels, but other factors such as age, smoking, mood and medicines also play a part.

Can sexual problems improve with better blood sugar control?

Better control and healthy habits protect nerves and vessels over time, and may help. Results vary. A doctor can check for other causes and treat them.

Is erectile dysfunction a sign of heart disease?

It can be an early warning sign, so a doctor should check your blood pressure, cholesterol, sugar and heart health. It does not mean you have heart disease, but it is worth checking.

Are Viagra-type tablets safe for people with diabetes?

Many men with diabetes can use them, but only after a doctor reviews their medicines and heart. They must not be taken with nitrate medicines such as nitroglycerin, and the label also warns about low blood pressure with some other medicines.

Do women with diabetes have sexual problems too?

Yes. A review of 26 studies found that sexual problems are more common in women with diabetes. They are treatable, and a gynaecologist or your doctor can help.

Are ayurvedic or online 'power' products safe?

We cannot say that unregulated products are safe. Some may contain hidden drugs or interact with your medicines. Use only what a qualified doctor prescribes.
