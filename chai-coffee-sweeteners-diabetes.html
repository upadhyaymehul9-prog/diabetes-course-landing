Chai, Coffee and Sugar Substitutes With Diabetes: What Is Actually Okay?

You do not have to give up your morning chai. But the sugar in it, the biscuit next to it, and the third cup at 5 pm matter more than the tea leaves.

Short answer: the tea or coffee is rarely the problem. The sugar, the sweet biscuit and the number of cups are. Large studies link regular coffee drinking with a lower chance of developing type 2 diabetes, but that does not mean coffee treats diabetes. The WHO advises against using sugar substitutes to control weight, but says its advice does not apply to people who already have diabetes. The safest plan is to cut the sweetness down gradually and check your own readings.

Your chai is mostly a sugar question

For many people with diabetes, the first worry is chai. The honest answer is that plain tea with a little milk adds very little sugar to your day. What adds up is what goes into the cup.

A level teaspoon of sugar is about 4 grams. Here is how it adds up in a normal day:

HabitAdded sugar
3 cups of chai a day, 2 teaspoons each6 teaspoons, about 24 g
4 cups of chai a day, 2 teaspoons each8 teaspoons, about 32 g
Same 3 cups, with a biscuit or rusk each timeThe 24 g, plus the refined flour and sugar in 3 biscuits

Nobody counts this, because it happens in small spoons through the day. But for someone whose after-meal sugar is already high, 24 to 32 grams of sugar in drinks alone is a lot to carry. It also arrives without any fibre, protein or fat to slow it down.

Three small things make the biggest difference:

  • Reduce the sugar step by step. Go from 2 spoons to 1½, then 1, then ½. Your taste adjusts in a few weeks, and a sudden stop is the one people usually fail at.
  • Take the biscuit out first. The rusk, biscuit or namkeen next to the cup often does more harm than the sugar in it. Try a few roasted nuts or roasted chana instead.
  • Count the cups. Three or four sweet cups can turn into six during a stressful workday. Our pages on sugar cravings and on stress, sleep and blood sugar explain why the 4 pm cup is so hard to skip.
A note on jaggery, honey and "natural" sweeteners. People often switch from white sugar to jaggery, honey or brown sugar and think the problem is solved. These still add sugar to your drink. They do not make a sweet cup safe for diabetes. Treat them as sugar and measure them the same way.

What the research says about coffee

Coffee has one of the more positive research stories in nutrition, but it is easy to misread.

A 2018 systematic review with meta-analysis in Nutrition Reviews brought together studies on coffee drinking and the risk of developing type 2 diabetes. It found that higher coffee intake was associated with a lower risk of developing the condition. The authors discuss possible reasons, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, but the reasons are not settled.1

What this does not mean

  • It does not show that coffee prevents diabetes. These were observational studies. People who drink coffee may differ from people who do not in other ways, such as diet and activity.
  • It is about developing diabetes, not treating it. The review looked at the chance of getting type 2 diabetes. It does not show that coffee lowers blood sugar in a person who already has diabetes.
  • It assumes plain coffee. A cup with two spoons of sugar or a large milky, sweetened café drink is a different thing from black coffee or coffee with a splash of milk.
  • It is not a reason to start drinking coffee. If you do not drink it, you do not need to begin.

The practical message is reassuring but modest: if you already drink coffee, you do not need to quit it because of diabetes. Keep it unsweetened or lightly sweetened, and watch the total number of cups, because caffeine late in the day can hurt sleep, and poor sleep raises blood sugar. See stress, sleep and blood sugar.

We did not find strong evidence in what we reviewed to say that any specific herbal tea, green tea or "diabetes tea" lowers blood sugar. Be careful with products that claim it does. See common diabetes myths.

What about sugar substitutes?

Many people move from sugar to a sweetener in the tea: stevia drops, sucralose or aspartame tablets, or "sugar-free" powders. It feels like a clean solution. The evidence is less clean.

In May 2023 the World Health Organization (WHO) issued a guideline on non-sugar sweeteners. It advises against using them to control body weight or to reduce the risk of non-communicable diseases. The WHO said that replacing free sugars with these sweeteners does not help with weight control in the long term, and it suggested that people reduce the sweetness of their diet overall instead.2 The sweeteners it names include aspartame, acesulfame K, saccharin, sucralose and stevia.2

Read the fine print: the WHO recommendation applies to the general population except people who already have diabetes.2 So it is not a rule telling people with diabetes to stop. But it also does not say that sweeteners are a healthy habit. It says they do not help with long-term weight control, and weight control is a central goal in type 2 diabetes.

What this means in daily life:

  • A sugar substitute is a useful tool if it helps you cut sugar while you retrain your taste. It should not become a way to eat more sweet food in general.
  • "Sugar-free" does not mean "carbohydrate-free." A sugar-free mithai or biscuit can still be made with refined flour, dates, ghee and starch, and it can still raise your sugar. Always check your own reading two hours later.
  • The WHO's wider message is to slowly reduce how sweet your food and drinks taste. That works better in the long run than swapping one sweet taste for another.

We cannot rank the sweeteners from best to worst, because the sources we used do not do so. If you are unsure which one suits you, ask your doctor or a dietitian, especially if you also have kidney or liver problems.

A simple plan for your cups

  1. Measure for three days. Count your cups and how much sugar goes into each. Most people are surprised.
  2. Cut the sugar in half over three to four weeks. Then halve it again if you can.
  3. Break the pairing. Do not have the cup with a biscuit, a rusk or fried snacks. Pair it with protein, such as nuts, a boiled egg or roasted chana.
  4. Fix a cut-off time. A last caffeinated cup around midday or early afternoon protects your sleep.
  5. Test the difference. Check your sugar two hours after a sweet cup with a biscuit, and again after an unsweetened cup. Seeing the numbers helps more than any advice. Use our guide to tracking your numbers.
  6. Do not skip meals for tea. If you take insulin or tablets that can cause low sugar, replacing a meal with tea can be risky. Read about low blood sugar.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Can I drink chai if I have diabetes?

Yes, for most people. The problem is usually the sugar, the biscuit with it and the number of cups, not the tea. Cut the sugar down gradually, skip the biscuit and check your own sugar two hours later.

Is coffee good for diabetes?

A 2018 review of studies linked regular coffee drinking with a lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes. That does not mean coffee treats diabetes, and sweetened coffee drinks are a different matter. If you already drink coffee, you do not need to stop. If you do not, there is no need to start.

Is stevia or sucralose safe for people with diabetes?

The WHO's 2023 guideline advises against non-sugar sweeteners for weight control in the general population, but says this does not apply to people with pre-existing diabetes. They can help you cut sugar, but they do not help weight control in the long term. Ask your doctor which one suits you.

Is jaggery or honey better than sugar in tea?

No. They still add sugar to your drink and can raise your blood sugar. Treat them like sugar and measure them the same way.

Does green tea lower blood sugar?

We did not find strong enough evidence in the sources we reviewed to say that green tea or any herbal tea lowers blood sugar. They are fine as unsweetened drinks, but do not rely on them as a treatment.

How much sugar is in my daily chai?

A level teaspoon is about 4 grams. Three cups of chai with two teaspoons each is 6 teaspoons, about 24 grams of added sugar a day, before any biscuits.
