Alcohol and Type 2 Diabetes: What Is Safe, What Is Not, and the Low-Sugar Trap

Many people with diabetes drink at weddings, parties or with friends. Here is what the guidance says, without a lecture.

Short answer: if you do not drink, do not start. If you do drink, the ADA advises staying within daily limits: no more than 1 drink a day for women and 2 for men. The WHO says no amount of alcohol is completely safe for health. The special danger in diabetes is low blood sugar, even many hours later, especially if you take insulin or certain tablets. Never drink on an empty stomach.

What the guidance says

The ADA 2026 Standards of Care advise adults with diabetes who drink alcohol to stay within recommended daily limits. It also says to advise people who do not drink not to start.1

The limits it quotes from US dietary guidelines are up to 1 drink a day for women and up to 2 drinks a day for men. The ADA also notes that the World Health Organization says there is no safe amount of alcohol for health.1 So these are upper limits, not targets.

One standard drink is about
Beer12 oz (about 355 ml)
Wine5 oz (about 150 ml)
Spirits (whisky, rum, vodka)1.5 oz (about 45 ml)

Note that a "large peg" at a bar or a party is often more than one drink.

The low-sugar trap

Your liver normally releases sugar into the blood to keep it steady between meals. Alcohol interferes with this. So a drink can cause low blood sugar, and the ADA warns that this can come hours later, for example overnight.1

The ADA advises people with diabetes to be counselled about delayed low sugar and to check their glucose after drinking, especially if they take insulin or insulin secretagogues (tablets that push the body to make more insulin, such as sulfonylureas).1

Why this is dangerous: the signs of low sugar, such as confusion, sweating, shaking and slurred speech, can look like being drunk. Other people may not help, and you may not notice yourself. Read our guide on low blood sugar and make sure someone with you knows you have diabetes.

Other risks to know about

  • Weight gain. Alcohol adds calories, and snacks eaten with it add more. The ADA lists weight gain as a risk.1 See weight loss and belly fat.
  • High blood sugar with heavy drinking. The ADA notes excessive amounts can raise sugar.1
  • Interaction with some medicines. The ADA advises avoiding excessive alcohol with SGLT2 inhibitors, a group of diabetes medicines.1 Ask your doctor how alcohol fits with the medicines you take, including metformin.
  • Your liver. Alcohol and fatty liver are a bad mix. Read about fatty liver and diabetes.
  • Blood fats and blood pressure. Alcohol can push both up. See diabetes and cholesterol and blood pressure.

Not sure which group your tablets belong to? Show your prescription to your doctor or pharmacist and ask.

Weddings, parties and peer pressure

The hard part is rarely the drink. It is the situation: a late dinner, many rounds, snacks of fried food and sweets, and friends who say "one more".

  • Decide your limit before you arrive, and say it simply: "I'm good with this one."
  • Hold a glass of water or soda water. A full hand is rarely questioned.
  • Eat first, and pick protein and vegetables over fried snacks and sweets. See food order and festival season.
  • Carry a fast sugar source if you use insulin or sulfonylureas, such as glucose tablets or a small juice pack, in case of a low.
  • Do not drive after drinking, and do not assume that feeling "fine" means your sugar is fine.
  • Plan the next morning. Check sugar, eat a normal breakfast and take your medicines as usual.

If you do drink

  1. Never drink on an empty stomach. Eat a proper meal with the drink.
  2. Keep it within the limit, and measure. Count a standard drink, not a glass.
  3. Go slowly. Alternate with water.
  4. Avoid sweet mixers. Cola, juice and sweet cocktails add a lot of sugar. See sugar, jaggery and honey.
  5. Check your sugar before bed and have a snack if the number is low or falling. Check again in the morning. Ask your doctor what your safe bedtime range is.
  6. Do not skip medicine or meals because you plan to drink.
  7. Tell a friend or family member. They should know what to do if you become confused or drowsy.

When it is better not to drink

  • You are pregnant or trying to become pregnant.
  • You have liver disease, pancreatitis, or very high triglycerides.
  • You often have low sugar episodes, or you have nerve damage that you notice more after drinking.
  • You are driving. Alcohol and low sugar together are a serious risk.
  • You find it hard to stop at one drink. Talk to your doctor. This is common and help is available.

If you do not drink now, there is no health reason to start.1

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Can I drink alcohol if I have type 2 diabetes?

The ADA says adults with diabetes who drink should stay within daily limits, and that people who do not drink should not start. The WHO says no amount is completely safe for health. Ask your doctor whether alcohol is safe with your medicines.

How much alcohol is allowed?

The ADA quotes up to 1 drink a day for women and up to 2 for men. One drink is about 355 ml beer, 150 ml wine or 45 ml spirits. These are upper limits, not goals.

Can alcohol cause low blood sugar in diabetes?

Yes. It can cause low blood sugar, including hours after you drink, for example overnight. The risk is higher with insulin or insulin-boosting tablets. Check your glucose after drinking.

Is beer or whisky better for diabetes?

We did not find evidence that one type is safer. What matters is the amount, what you eat with it, and what you mix it with. Sweet mixers and snacks add sugar and calories.

Can I drink with metformin?

Ask your doctor, who knows your full medicine list. The ADA advises avoiding excess alcohol in general, and specifically with SGLT2 inhibitors.

Does alcohol raise blood sugar?

Heavy drinking can raise blood sugar, and the extra calories can lead to weight gain. In small amounts it more often causes low sugar later. Both are risks.
