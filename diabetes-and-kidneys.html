The Silent Complication

Diabetes and Your Kidneys: The Two Tests That Catch Damage Early

Kidney damage from diabetes usually gives no warning in the early years. Two simple tests find it while it can still be slowed. Most people never ask for them.

By Dr. Mehul Upadhyay · Last updated 20 November 2026

Short answer: the American Diabetes Association recommends that everyone with type 2 diabetes has their kidneys checked with two tests: a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, worked out from a blood creatinine test). Early kidney damage has no symptoms, so the tests are the only way to catch it. If you have diabetes and cannot remember your last UACR, ask for it at your next visit.

Why diabetes affects the kidneys

Your kidneys are two filters. Each one holds about a million tiny filtering units with very fine blood vessels. They clean waste from your blood and keep useful things, like protein, in the body.

Over time, high blood sugar and high blood pressure can damage these tiny vessels. The filters begin to leak. The first thing to leak is a protein called albumin, and it shows up in the urine. If the damage continues, the kidneys slowly lose their ability to filter waste. Doctors call this diabetic kidney disease, and it is one cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD).

The key fact is that early kidney damage does not cause symptoms. You do not feel pain or discomfort. You may feel completely well while the damage progresses. The ADA states that kidney disease may already be present when type 2 diabetes is first diagnosed.1 This is why screening, not symptoms, is the plan.

Think of it like this: the eyes, kidneys and feet can all be damaged quietly. Checking them regularly is how you stay ahead. See our overview of diabetes complications, and the pages on eye health and foot care.

The two tests you should know by name

The ADA recommends assessing kidney function with two tests, in all people with type 2 diabetes, whatever medicine they take.1 They look at different things, and you need both.

TestWhat it measuresHow it is done
UACR
(urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio)		How much albumin is leaking into the urine. It is the earliest sign of kidney damage.A single urine sample, often a spot sample. No special preparation is usually needed.
eGFR
(estimated glomerular filtration rate)		How well the kidneys are filtering. It is calculated from your blood creatinine together with your age and sex.A blood test. Many labs report the eGFR automatically with the creatinine result.

A common mistake is to rely on blood creatinine alone. Creatinine can look normal while the kidneys are already leaking albumin. The urine test (UACR) is the one that picks up the early problem. Ask for both.

How often?

The ADA says to check at diagnosis for type 2 diabetes. For people who already have chronic kidney disease, it advises monitoring urine albumin and eGFR one to four times a year, depending on the stage.1 For people without kidney disease, a yearly check is the usual standard. Your doctor will set the schedule that fits you.

A basic lab package may list "creatinine" and "urine routine" without a UACR. If your report has no UACR or albumin result, ask for it by name.

What your results mean

Doctors place your result on two scales together: the albumin level and the eGFR. Here are the albumin categories that the ADA uses:1

UACR (mg/g)CategoryMeaning
Below 30Normal to mildly increasedNo sign of leaking. Keep checking regularly.
30 to 299Moderately increasedEarly kidney damage. This used to be called "microalbuminuria."
300 or moreSeverely increasedMore advanced damage. Needs close medical care.

An eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² also points to reduced kidney function. Doctors usually want a raised UACR or a low eGFR to be present on repeat testing before they call it kidney disease, because one result can be affected by fever, a hard workout or a urine infection. So a single abnormal result is a reason to repeat, not to panic.

If a result is abnormal, the next step is to see your doctor or a kidney specialist soon, not to restrict foods on your own. Some diet changes help kidneys, but the right ones depend on your stage and other health problems.

Late-stage kidney problems can cause swelling in the ankles, feet or face, or foamy urine. Do not wait for these. They usually show up after the early window for protecting the kidneys has passed.

What helps protect your kidneys

The aim after an abnormal result, or if you want to prevent one, is to slow or stop the damage. The ADA gives a clear first step: optimise blood glucose management to reduce the risk or slow the progression of kidney disease.1 Beyond sugar control, these things matter:

  • Blood pressure control. High pressure speeds up kidney damage. Most people with diabetes and high pressure need a clear target and regular checks. See diabetes and blood pressure.
  • The right medicines, chosen by your doctor. The ADA recommends an SGLT2 inhibitor for many people with type 2 diabetes and CKD, and an ACE inhibitor or ARB for people with high blood pressure and raised urine albumin.1 These are prescription decisions. Do not start or stop them yourself.
  • Care with painkillers. Some common pain tablets can harm the kidneys when used often. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before you take them regularly.
  • Do not stop your diabetes medicines on your own. Metformin is not always unsafe in kidney disease, but the dose may change as kidney function changes. See our page on why you should never adjust your own medication.
  • A healthy weight, regular movement and good sleep. These improve sugar and pressure at the same time. They are the core of the 12-week plan.
  • No smoking. It adds to kidney and blood vessel damage.

The international guideline on this topic is the KDIGO 2022 guideline for diabetes management in chronic kidney disease.2 It covers glucose targets, lifestyle, medicines and care models for people with both conditions. Your doctor will use it, or something like it, to plan your care.

The goal is early detection. The earlier damage is found, the more options you have and the more you can slow it down.

A five-minute checklist for your next doctor visit

Kidney checks only help if the results are read and acted on. Many people have the tests done and never hear what the numbers were. Use this list at your next visit.

  1. Ask for the dates and numbers. When was my last UACR? When was my last eGFR? What were the values, and are they getting better or worse?
  2. Bring your last two reports. The trend matters more than any single result. A UACR that is creeping up, or an eGFR that is slowly falling, is worth acting on even if each number is still "in range."
  3. Check the units. Labs may report UACR in mg/g or in mg/mmol. The categories above are in mg/g. If your report uses another unit, ask your doctor or the lab to interpret it.
  4. Ask about your blood pressure target and how often it should be checked at home.
  5. Ask whether any of your medicines need a dose change as your kidney function changes, and whether any you take regularly could affect the kidneys.
  6. Ask whether you need a kidney specialist (nephrologist), especially if your UACR is raised or your eGFR is falling.
Keep a simple record. Write the date, UACR, eGFR, blood pressure and HbA1c in one place each time. Our tracking guide shows how to keep your numbers so the pattern becomes visible. Pair it with a yearly eye check and a regular foot check, because the same blood sugar that affects the kidneys also affects those organs.

You do not need to be afraid of these tests. They are quick, cheap compared with the cost of late treatment, and they give you and your doctor the chance to protect your kidneys while there is still a lot you can do.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Does everyone with diabetes get kidney disease?

No. Many people with diabetes never develop it, and good control of blood sugar and blood pressure lowers the risk. But it is common enough that every person with type 2 diabetes should be screened.

What are the two tests for diabetic kidney disease?

A urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), which finds albumin leaking into the urine, and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), which is calculated from a blood creatinine test and shows how well the kidneys filter. You need both.

How often should kidneys be checked if I have type 2 diabetes?

The American Diabetes Association recommends checking at diagnosis and then regularly, with a yearly check being the common standard. If kidney disease is found, monitoring becomes one to four times a year depending on the stage. Your doctor will set the schedule.

Is a normal creatinine enough?

No. Creatinine can look normal while albumin is already leaking into the urine. That is why the urine test (UACR) is needed as well as the blood test.

Can kidney damage from diabetes be reversed?

Treatment aims to slow or stop the damage, and early detection gives the best chance. Advanced damage cannot be undone, which is why catching it early matters so much.

Are there symptoms of early kidney damage?

Usually none. Later signs can include swelling of the feet, ankles or face and foamy urine, but these tend to appear after the early window has passed. Do not wait for symptoms to get tested.
