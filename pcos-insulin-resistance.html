PCOS and Insulin Resistance: The Diabetes Link and What Helps

PCOS is more than a period problem. It is also a metabolic condition. Here is what the evidence says.

By Dr. Mehul Upadhyay · Last updated 27 November 2026

Short answer: PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) has metabolic features, including risk factors for heart disease.1 An updated guideline says insulin resistance with high insulin levels is present in about 85% of those affected.2 Insulin resistance raises the chance of type 2 diabetes. The 2023 international guideline puts healthy lifestyle at the centre of care.1 Medicines such as metformin are for your doctor to decide.

What PCOS is

PCOS affects an estimated 10 to 13% of women worldwide, based on the international guideline process.3 It combines reproductive, metabolic and psychological features.3 Typical signs are irregular periods, extra hair growth and acne, but not everyone has all of them. A doctor makes the diagnosis.

The link with insulin resistance and diabetes

The 2023 international guideline highlights the broader picture of PCOS, including metabolic risk factors, cardiovascular disease, sleep apnoea, and a high burden of psychological features.1 The 2026 update to the guideline text describes insulin resistance with high insulin levels as present in 85% of those affected.2

That is why women with PCOS should have their blood sugar checked, even if they are young or not overweight. See HbA1c explained, fasting vs after-meal sugar and type 2 diabetes before 40.

Lifestyle comes first

The guideline emphasises a healthy lifestyle, emotional wellbeing and quality of life, and it asks clinicians to be aware of weight stigma.1 In practice that means a plan you can keep, not a crash diet.

  • Food: whole foods, dal, vegetables, less sugar and refined flour. See what to eat and an Indian diet chart.
  • Movement: regular activity you enjoy. See how much exercise.
  • Sleep and stress: see stress, sleep and blood sugar.
  • Waist: see belly fat.

Be kind to yourself. Weight is only one part of PCOS, and the guideline itself warns about weight stigma.1

What about metformin?

A systematic review of randomised trials prepared for the 2023 guideline compared metformin with placebo in PCOS. Metformin gave a larger fall in BMI (about 0.5 kg/m²), in insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and in fasting glucose (about 0.13 mmol/L).4 The certainty of the evidence was moderate.4

Whether you need metformin, and at what dose, is for your doctor. Do not start or stop it yourself. See metformin for diabetes for how it works and its side effects.

A note on the new name

In May 2026 the guideline was updated to use a new name, polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), formerly PCOS. The recommendations were unchanged.2 Many doctors and women still say PCOS.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Is PCOS linked to diabetes?

Yes. The updated international guideline says insulin resistance is present in about 85% of those affected, and insulin resistance raises the chance of type 2 diabetes.

Can lean women have PCOS and insulin resistance?

Yes. PCOS is not limited to women with extra weight. Ask your doctor to check your blood sugar either way.

Does lifestyle help PCOS?

The 2023 international guideline puts healthy lifestyle at the centre of care, along with emotional wellbeing.

Does metformin help PCOS?

In trials it gave larger falls in BMI, insulin resistance and fasting glucose than placebo. Your doctor decides if it is right for you.

Is PCOS being renamed?

Yes. A May 2026 update to the international guideline uses polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), formerly PCOS. The recommendations did not change.

Which tests should I ask for?

Ask about HbA1c or glucose testing, blood pressure and cholesterol, in addition to the tests for your hormones.
