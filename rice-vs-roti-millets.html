Rice, Roti or Millets? What the Evidence Says for Type 2 Diabetes

Almost everyone asks which one is safer. The honest answer is less simple than "roti good, rice bad" — and millets help, but not in the way marketing says.

Short answer: in a large review of studies, white rice and refined wheat had almost the same glycaemic index. Roti is not automatically safer than rice. Swapping part of your refined grain for millets such as ragi, bajra or jowar lowered blood sugar in people with diabetes. But portion size, what you eat with the grain, and your own after-meal reading matter just as much.

Is roti really better than rice for diabetes?

In most Indian homes the advice is the same: "eat roti, avoid rice." It sounds sensible. But when researchers measure how fast a food raises blood sugar, the picture is different.

The glycaemic index (GI) is a score from 0 to 100 that shows how quickly a food raises blood glucose compared with pure glucose. A higher number means a faster rise. A large 2021 review of millet studies compared millets against the staples most of us eat every day. It reported an average GI of about 71.7 for milled (polished) rice and about 74.2 for refined wheat.1 Those two numbers are close. Both are in the high range.

So if your roti is made from fine, refined wheat flour, it can raise your sugar about as quickly as a plate of white rice. The review compared millets with refined wheat, so it does not give a single number for every kind of roti. Whole-grain flour keeps more fibre, and that is why it is the usual advice. But the label "roti" alone does not make a meal safe.

What this means for you: do not judge a meal only by its name. Judge it by how much you eat, what you eat it with, and what your sugar does two hours later.

This is also why one person says "rice spikes me" and another says "roti spikes me." Bodies respond differently. The only way to know your own answer is to test it, and we show how in the next sections.

What the glycaemic index can and cannot tell you

GI is useful, but it is not the whole story. It measures a single food on its own. Real meals are mixed. A roti eaten with dal, sabzi and curd behaves differently from the same roti eaten alone or with pickle and tea.

Three things change what a grain does to your sugar:

  • How much you eat. A bigger portion of any grain means more total carbohydrate, and more glucose in the blood.
  • What else is on the plate. Vegetables, protein and curd slow digestion. Our page on whether the order you eat in matters goes into this.
  • How it is made. Fine flour, long cooking and mashing generally make a food digest faster than coarse, whole, less-processed forms.

The practical lesson: a smaller portion of any grain, eaten after vegetables and protein, is a better plan than a large portion of the "right" grain eaten alone.

What millets actually do for blood sugar

Millets are small-seeded grains that India has eaten for thousands of years: ragi (finger millet), bajra (pearl millet), jowar (sorghum), and others such as foxtail, barnyard, kodo and little millet.

The 2021 review in Frontiers in Nutrition pooled the results of many studies. Here is what it found, in plain terms.1

FindingWhat the review reported
GI compared with white rice and refined wheatAll the millets had a significantly lower GI than white rice or refined wheat, except little millet, where the results were inconsistent.
Intermediate-GI milletsPearl millet (bajra), finger millet (ragi), kodo millet, little millet and sorghum (jowar) fall in the 55–69 range. That is 13–35% lower than a high-GI control food.
Low-GI milletsFoxtail and barnyard millet, along with Job's tears, fonio and teff, had the lowest GI values.
People with diabetes who ate millets over the long termFasting glucose fell by about 12% and after-meal glucose by about 15%. In the review's figures, fasting glucose went from 134 to about 118 mg/dL and after-meal glucose from 202 to about 172 mg/dL.

That is a real and useful effect. It is the kind of change that can move a person from "high" toward "better," especially when it is combined with weight loss and walking.

What this does not prove

  • It is not a cure. An average fall of 12–15% helps, but it does not by itself bring most people with diabetes to normal numbers.
  • The studies were mixed. They used different millets, different amounts, different durations and different people. Averages hide a wide range.
  • Not every millet is low GI. Ragi and jowar sit in the intermediate range. They are better than white rice, but they are not "free" foods.
  • The effect comes from replacing refined grain. Adding millets on top of what you already eat does not lower your sugar.
Watch the packaging. "Millet cookies," "ragi biscuits" and "multigrain" snacks are often made mostly from refined flour, sugar and oil. The word millet on the front does not make them a diabetes-friendly food. Read the ingredient list and check your own reading after eating one.

Practical swaps that do not feel like punishment

The research supports one clear direction: replace part of your refined grain with millets or whole grains. It does not require you to give up rice or roti completely. Below are steps most people can actually keep up. These are practical suggestions, not study results.

  1. Change one meal first. Pick the meal where you eat the most grain, often dinner. Swap that grain for a millet version for two weeks before you change anything else.
  2. Mix the flour. If a pure millet roti is hard to enjoy, start with your usual wheat flour and mix in ragi, bajra, jowar or besan (chickpea flour). Increase the millet share slowly as your taste adjusts.
  3. Try millet in place of rice. Foxtail, barnyard, kodo and little millet cook like rice, in a pot or cooker. They work well in khichdi, pulao and curd rice.
  4. Keep the portion visible. Use the plate method on our diabetes diet chart: half the plate vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter grain.
  5. Start the meal with vegetables and protein. Eat the dal, sabzi, egg, paneer or curd first, and the grain last.
  6. Walk after the meal. Ten to fifteen minutes helps. See our guide to walking after meals.

If you follow a vegetarian diet, make sure the grain swap does not leave you short on protein. Our vegetarian diabetes page covers the protein question, and the Indian diet page shows how to build a full plate without a Western diet.

Test your own response, then decide

The most useful experiment costs very little. Eat your usual meal, then check your blood sugar two hours after you start eating. A few days later, eat the new version (a smaller portion, or a millet swap) and check again at the same time. Write both numbers down.

For the targets that mark "normal" and "high" after meals, see fasting vs after-meal sugar. For a simple way to record the numbers, see our page on tracking your numbers.

If the new version gives you a lower reading, keep it. If it does not, change the portion or the pairing before you change the grain again. Your own numbers are better evidence than any chart.

A safety note about medicines

If you take insulin or tablets that can cause low blood sugar, changing how many carbohydrates you eat can change your dose needs. Talk to your doctor before you make a big change, and learn the signs of a low in our page on low blood sugar. Never reduce or stop your medicine because you started eating millets. Our page on why you should not adjust your own medication explains why.

Four common mistakes

  • Treating brown rice or millets as unlimited. A large bowl of any grain still raises your sugar.
  • Going "roti only" and eating five or six. The count of rotis adds up faster than the count of rice spoons.
  • Eating the grain with tea and biscuits, or with only pickle. A grain-heavy plate with little protein gives the biggest rise.
  • Changing everything on day one. Large sudden changes rarely last. One meal at a time does.
Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Is roti better than rice for diabetes?

Not automatically. In a large 2021 review, refined wheat and milled rice had similar glycaemic index values (about 74 and 72). Whole-grain flour, a smaller portion and eating vegetables and protein first matter more than the name of the grain.

Are ragi, bajra and jowar good for diabetes?

They are a better choice than white rice or refined wheat. In the review, ragi, bajra and jowar had a lower glycaemic index, and long-term millet eating lowered fasting glucose by about 12% and after-meal glucose by about 15% in people with diabetes. They are not free foods, so portion size still matters.

Which millet is best for blood sugar?

Foxtail and barnyard millet had the lowest glycaemic index values in the review. Ragi, bajra, jowar and kodo are in the intermediate range, which is still lower than white rice. Little millet gave inconsistent results.

How many rotis can a person with diabetes eat?

There is no single number that fits everyone. It depends on your weight, your medicines, how active you are and your own after-meal readings. Start with the plate method (a quarter of the plate for grain), eat protein and vegetables first, and check your sugar two hours after eating.

Will eating millets cure my diabetes?

No. Millets can lower blood sugar when they replace refined grains, but they are one part of a plan that also needs weight loss, movement, sleep and regular medical care.

Is brown rice better than white rice?

The review we used looked at millets, not brown rice, so we cannot give you a number for it. Brown rice keeps more fibre than white rice, but it is still a grain. Test your own reading two hours after a measured portion.
