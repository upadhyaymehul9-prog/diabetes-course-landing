Heart Protection

Diabetes and Cholesterol: What to Test, What to Aim For, and Whether You Need a Statin

High sugar and high cholesterol often travel together. Both harm your heart and blood vessels. Here is what the current guidance says, in simple words.

Short answer: if you have type 2 diabetes, your cholesterol should be checked when you are diagnosed and then every year if you are not on treatment. Lifestyle changes help everyone. Many people with diabetes aged 40 to 75 are also advised to take a statin, because it lowers the risk of heart attack and stroke. This is a decision for you and your doctor. Do not start or stop a statin on your own.

Why cholesterol matters more when you have diabetes

Diabetes damages blood vessels over time. Extra cholesterol in the blood can build up inside those vessels and narrow them. Together they raise the risk of heart attack and stroke. This is the main reason doctors check your lipids (blood fats) along with your sugar.

Diabetes often changes the pattern of blood fats too. Many people have high triglycerides and low HDL ("good") cholesterol. Better blood sugar control, weight loss and exercise can help improve this pattern.1

You will not feel high cholesterol. There is no symptom. Only a blood test shows it.

Which tests, and how often

The ADA 2026 Standards of Care say:1

  • Get a lipid profile when diabetes is diagnosed and at your first full evaluation.
  • If you are not on cholesterol medicine, repeat it every year.
  • If you are on a statin, it is checked when you start, about 4 to 12 weeks after you start or change the dose, and then every year.
MeasureCommon targets in the ADA standards
LDL ("bad") cholesterolBelow 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L) for many people aged 40 to 75 at higher risk. Below 55 mg/dL (1.4 mmol/L) for people who already have heart or vessel disease.1 Your doctor sets your own target.
TriglyceridesThe ADA treats a fasting level of 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) or more as a signal to look harder at lifestyle and sugar control.1
HDL ("good") cholesterolBelow 40 mg/dL in men, or below 50 mg/dL in women, is a signal to intensify lifestyle.1

These numbers are from the ADA guidance for adults with diabetes. Indian guidelines may set slightly different goals. Ask your doctor for your own target, because it depends on your age, other risk factors and whether you already have heart disease. What you can do yourself

The ADA recommends lifestyle change for lipids in every person with diabetes. Its list includes:1

  • Lose weight if you carry extra. Our guide on weight loss with diabetes and the guide on belly fat show how.
  • Follow a Mediterranean-style or DASH-style eating pattern. In Indian terms: more vegetables, dals, nuts, fish or other lean protein, and less fried food and sweets. See the Indian diet guide.
  • Eat less saturated fat and trans fat. That means less ghee-heavy gravies, fried snacks and bakery items made with vanaspati or "partially hydrogenated" oil.
  • Eat more omega-3 and soluble fibre. Think oats, beans, lentils, flaxseed and fatty fish.
  • Be more active. Even a short walk after meals helps. See post-meal walk and movement.
Good to know: the same habits that help your sugar also help your cholesterol and fatty liver. See fatty liver and diabetes. You do not need a separate plan.

Do you need a statin?

Statins are tablets that lower LDL cholesterol. In people with diabetes, the ADA 2026 standards say:1

  • Age 40 to 75, no heart disease: a moderate-intensity statin is recommended, along with lifestyle.
  • Age 40 to 75 with higher risk: a high-intensity statin is recommended, aiming to cut LDL by at least half and reach below 70 mg/dL.
  • Age 20 to 39 with extra risk factors: a statin may be reasonable.
  • Over 75: if you already take a statin, continue it. If you do not, starting a moderate-intensity statin may be reasonable.
  • If LDL stays high on the best statin dose you can take: adding another medicine, such as ezetimibe, may be reasonable.

These are guidelines for groups of people. Your doctor looks at your own age, blood pressure, smoking, kidney health and family history. See also diabetes and your kidneys.

Do not start or stop a statin on your own. If you have muscle pain or other side effects on a statin, tell your doctor. There are often options, such as a different dose or a different statin. Stopping silently removes the heart protection you were getting.

A word on triglycerides

Triglycerides are another type of blood fat. They rise with extra sugar, refined carbohydrates, alcohol and extra weight. For most people the first steps are the same: cut sweet drinks and sweets, reduce refined foods, limit alcohol, lose weight and stay active.1 See our pages on sugar cravings and alcohol and diabetes.

Very high triglycerides, 500 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or more, need a doctor's evaluation because they can bring a risk of pancreatitis.1 Do not try to manage that range on your own.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

How often should I check cholesterol if I have diabetes?

The ADA advises a lipid profile at diagnosis, and every year after that if you are not on cholesterol medicine. If you take a statin, it is checked 4 to 12 weeks after you start or change the dose, and then yearly.

What LDL target is right for me?

It depends on your age and risk. For many people with diabetes aged 40 to 75 at higher risk, the ADA suggests below 70 mg/dL. For people who already have heart or vessel disease, below 55 mg/dL. Your doctor will set your own number.

Do all people with diabetes need a statin?

Not all, but many do. The ADA recommends a moderate-intensity statin for most people with diabetes aged 40 to 75, and a stronger one for those at higher risk. Younger and older people are assessed case by case.

Can I lower cholesterol without medicine?

Lifestyle helps everyone: weight loss, a Mediterranean-style or DASH-style eating pattern, less saturated and trans fat, more fibre and omega-3, and more activity. Whether that is enough depends on your numbers and your overall risk. Your doctor decides.

What if I get muscle pain on a statin?

Tell your doctor before you stop. There are often other options, such as changing the dose or the statin.

Does a high triglyceride level mean I should stop all fat?

No. The usual steps are to cut sweet drinks, sweets and refined foods, limit alcohol, lose weight if needed and be more active. Your doctor will advise on anything more.
