Type 2 Diabetes Complications: What Can Go Wrong and How to Lower the Risk

Complications are not certain. Early control and regular checks lower the risk.

By Dr. Mehul Upadhyay · Last updated 27 November 2026

Short answer: long-term high blood sugar is the best-established risk factor for damage to the eyes, nerves and kidneys.1 In the UKPDS trial, people with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who had tighter control (HbA1c 7.0% against 7.9%) had fewer small-vessel complications, and some benefit lasted for 10 years after the trial.2,4 Get your yearly checks done.

The main complications

  • Eyes (retinopathy). Damage to the small vessels in the retina. See eye health.
  • Kidneys (nephropathy). See diabetes and kidneys.
  • Nerves (neuropathy). Numbness, tingling or pain, often in the feet. See foot care.
  • Heart and blood vessels. See blood pressure and cholesterol.

The first three are called small-vessel (microvascular) complications. Heart and large-vessel disease is a separate group.

Why they happen

The ADA says chronic high blood sugar is the best-established risk factor linked to microvascular complications such as retinopathy and neuropathy.1 Other risks add to it. For retinopathy these include how long you have had diabetes, kidney disease, high blood pressure and abnormal cholesterol.1

That is why blood pressure and cholesterol checks belong in your diabetes care, not only sugar tests.

Why early control matters: the UKPDS trial

UKPDS was the first large trial to compare two levels of blood sugar control in people with new type 2 diabetes. Over 10 years, HbA1c averaged 7.0% in the intensive group and 7.9% in the conventional group.2 The intensive group had fewer small-vessel complications, but not fewer large-vessel ones, during the trial.3

After the trial, the HbA1c gap closed within about a year. Even so, the lower small-vessel risk continued over 10 more years, and later reductions in heart attack and death appeared.4

Check your own number with HbA1c explained. Your doctor sets your target. The ADA's goal is below 7% for many adults, with higher or lower goals for some people.5

Yearly checks to ask for

CheckHow often
Dilated eye examAt diagnosis. If there is no damage and sugar is in range, every 1 to 2 years may be enough.1
Kidney tests (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio and eGFR)Yearly6
Foot exam (pulses, reflexes, feeling, monofilament)Yearly6
Blood pressureAt every visit6

Ask your own doctor for your schedule. It may be more frequent if you already have damage.

What you can do

  1. Keep your HbA1c in your target range. See newly diagnosed for the first steps.
  2. Lose weight if you carry extra. See weight loss and diabetes.
  3. Control blood pressure and cholesterol with your doctor.
  4. Check your feet daily. Report any sore that does not heal.
  5. Do not skip your yearly checks. Many complications have no symptoms early on.

Honest limits

  • UKPDS treated people with sulphonylureas, insulin or conventional diet, not with remission programmes. We do not have trial proof that remission itself prevents complications.
  • Risk differs from person to person. Some people with good control still get complications, and some with poor control do not.
  • This page cannot tell you your own risk. Your doctor can.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

What are the complications of type 2 diabetes?

The main ones are damage to the eyes, kidneys and nerves, and heart and blood-vessel disease. Foot problems often come from nerve and circulation damage.

Can diabetes complications be prevented?

The risk can be lowered. Staying in your HbA1c target, controlling blood pressure and cholesterol, and doing yearly checks all help.

How often should I get my eyes checked?

The ADA advises a dilated exam at diagnosis. If there is no damage and your sugar is in range, every 1 to 2 years may be considered.

Which tests check for kidney damage?

A urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio and an eGFR blood test, usually done yearly.

Do complications always cause symptoms?

No. Many are silent at first, which is why screening matters.

Does early control help years later?

In the UKPDS trial, people with earlier tighter control kept a lower small-vessel risk during 10 years of follow-up, even after their HbA1c levels matched.
