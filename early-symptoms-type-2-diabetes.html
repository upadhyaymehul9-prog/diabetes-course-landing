Know Before It Shows

Early Signs of Type 2 Diabetes — and Why Many People Have None

You can have high blood sugar for years and feel fine. Here are the signs that do appear, who should get tested even without them, and which tests give a real answer.

Short answer: early type 2 diabetes often has no symptoms at all. When symptoms do come, the usual ones are heavy thirst, passing urine often, tiredness, blurred vision and unexplained weight loss. The American Diabetes Association says people who carry extra weight and have a BMI of 23 or more (for people of Asian ancestry) should consider testing even if they feel well. Only a blood test can confirm diabetes.

Why type 2 diabetes is called a silent condition

Many people expect diabetes to announce itself. For type 2 diabetes it usually does not. Blood sugar rises slowly, over months and years. The body adjusts a little at a time, so there is no sudden day when you feel ill.

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) states in its 2026 Standards of Care that type 2 diabetes often goes undetected for years, because the high blood sugar develops gradually and in the early stages may not cause the classic symptoms.1

This matters because damage can start before you feel anything. High sugar over time affects the eyes, kidneys, nerves and blood vessels. By the time the signs are loud, the condition has often been present for a long while. That is why testing is more reliable than waiting for symptoms.

The main point: "I feel fine" is not a test result. If you have risk factors, check your blood sugar even when nothing seems wrong.

The signs that can appear

When symptoms do show up, they are linked to high glucose in the blood. The ADA lists the classic ones as passing a lot of urine (polyuria), being very thirsty (polydipsia) and unexplained weight loss.1 Doctors also ask about the following, which people often put down to stress, age or a busy life:

SignWhy it can happen
Passing urine often, including at nightWhen there is too much sugar in the blood, the kidneys try to remove it through the urine, and water goes with it.
Strong thirst that water does not fixYou lose fluid through frequent urination, so the body asks for more.
Tiredness, even after a full night's sleepThe cells cannot use sugar well for energy. See our page on why diabetes causes fatigue.
Blurred vision that comes and goesChanging sugar levels can affect the lens of the eye. See diabetic eye health.
Cuts and sores that heal slowly, or frequent infectionsHigh sugar can slow healing and make infections more likely. Check your feet often: foot care.
Tingling or numbness in hands or feetOver time, high sugar can irritate and damage nerves.
Darker, velvety skin at the neck or armpitsThis can be a sign of insulin resistance. See PCOS and insulin resistance.
Unexplained weight lossWhen the body cannot use sugar, it can start breaking down fat and muscle for energy.

None of these signs is proof of diabetes. Thirst can come from a hot day, and tiredness can come from poor sleep. What matters is a pattern, and especially a pattern that does not go away.

Go to a doctor or hospital urgently if high sugar comes with repeated vomiting, severe stomach pain, fast breathing, great drowsiness or confusion. These can be warning signs of a hyperglycaemic crisis such as diabetic ketoacidosis, which needs same-day care.

Who should get tested even without symptoms

Because the condition is so quiet, doctors do not wait for symptoms in people at higher risk. The ADA's 2026 guidance says testing should be considered in adults who are overweight or obese and have one or more risk factors. The BMI line it uses is 25 or above, and 23 or above for people of Asian ancestry. For everyone else, testing starts from age 35.1

The lower BMI line for Asian ancestry is important in India. It reflects that people of Asian ancestry tend to develop type 2 diabetes at lower body weights, and can carry fat around the waist even when the BMI looks normal. See our page on belly fat and diabetes.

Common risk factors that make testing more important include:

  • A parent, brother or sister with diabetes
  • A big waistline, or weight gain around the belly
  • High blood pressure or abnormal cholesterol (see diabetes and blood pressure)
  • Fatty liver (see fatty liver and diabetes)
  • PCOS, or diabetes during a past pregnancy
  • A mostly sitting lifestyle and poor sleep
  • A previous result in the prediabetes range

If you were diagnosed with prediabetes, read our prediabetes page. That stage is the best moment to act.

The tests that actually confirm it

Symptoms can raise suspicion, but diagnosis comes from a blood test. The ADA accepts any one of the following for a person who is not pregnant:1

TestDiabetesPrediabetes
HbA1c (average of about 3 months)6.5% or higher5.7% to 6.4%
Fasting plasma glucose (no food for at least 8 hours)126 mg/dL or higher100 to 125 mg/dL
2-hour glucose during a 75 g glucose tolerance test200 mg/dL or higher140 to 199 mg/dL
Random glucose, only with classic symptoms200 mg/dL or higher—

Without clear, unmistakable high sugar, the ADA says diagnosis needs two abnormal results, so your doctor may repeat the test.1 That is normal and not a sign that anyone made a mistake.

A home glucometer is a good tool for tracking, but a laboratory test is what confirms the diagnosis. To understand what each number on your report means, read our guides to HbA1c and to fasting vs after-meal sugar.

Getting the test done properly

A test is only useful if it is done correctly. These points help you get a result you can trust:

  • Fasting means fasting. For a fasting glucose test, the ADA definition is no calorie intake for at least 8 hours.1 A cup of sweet tea at 7 am means the sample is not a true fasting sample. Ask the lab what you may drink; plain water is usually allowed.
  • Do not change your habits just before the test. Eating very little for a few days, or skipping your usual meals, gives a result that does not reflect your normal life.
  • Ask the lab if you should take your regular medicines. Do not stop any on your own.
  • An oral glucose tolerance test takes about two hours. You drink a measured 75 g glucose drink and have blood drawn two hours later.1 Plan for the time.
  • HbA1c does not need fasting. It reflects the average of about three months, so one good morning will not hide a high result.
  • Repeat if told to. A second test is how doctors confirm, not a sign that anything went wrong.

Questions worth asking your doctor

  1. Which of these tests do I need, and how soon?
  2. Is my result normal, prediabetes or diabetes, by these cut-offs?
  3. Should I also have my kidneys, eyes, blood pressure and cholesterol checked? (See diabetes and your kidneys.)
  4. When should I repeat the test?
  5. What can I change in food, movement and sleep before we consider medicine?

What to do if your result is high

  1. Do not panic and do not self-treat. One high number needs confirming and explaining.
  2. See a doctor for confirmation and for a check of your kidneys, eyes, blood pressure and cholesterol.
  3. Start with the basics now: food structure, daily movement, sleep and weight. Our page for the newly diagnosed gives a calm first-week plan.
  4. Begin tracking. A few weeks of readings show patterns that a single test cannot. See tracking your numbers.
  5. Stay realistic. Many people improve a lot, and some reach remission, but it depends on the person. Our honest page on whether reversal is real explains what is and is not known.
Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Can I have type 2 diabetes and feel completely fine?

Yes. The American Diabetes Association notes that type 2 diabetes often goes undetected for years because blood sugar rises gradually and early on may not cause the classic symptoms. That is why testing matters if you have risk factors.

What are the first symptoms of type 2 diabetes?

The classic ones are heavy thirst, passing urine often and unexplained weight loss. Other common signs are tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing cuts, frequent infections and tingling in the hands or feet. Many people notice none of these.

At what BMI should an Indian adult get tested for diabetes?

The American Diabetes Association suggests considering testing in adults who are overweight and have a risk factor when BMI is 23 or higher for people of Asian ancestry (25 or higher for others). Without risk factors, testing starts from age 35. Ask your doctor what applies to you.

Which blood test confirms diabetes?

Any one of these: HbA1c of 6.5% or higher, fasting glucose of 126 mg/dL or higher, a 2-hour glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher on a glucose tolerance test, or a random glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher with classic symptoms. Without clear symptoms, a second abnormal result is needed.

Is passing urine often always a sign of diabetes?

No. It can also come from drinking a lot of fluid, caffeine, a urine infection or other conditions. It becomes more suspicious when it comes with strong thirst, tiredness or weight loss, and when it keeps happening.

Can a home glucometer diagnose diabetes?

A glucometer helps you track patterns, but a laboratory blood test is what confirms diabetes. If a home reading is high, repeat the test and see your doctor.
