Sweet Swaps

Sugar vs Jaggery vs Honey: Is Any of Them Better for Diabetes?

Many families switch from white sugar to gud or honey after a diabetes diagnosis. Here is why that switch may not help, and what does.

Short answer: for blood sugar, treat white sugar, jaggery (gud) and honey as the same thing: sugar. The WHO counts all of them as "free sugars". They may have a little extra minerals or taste, but we did not find good human trials showing jaggery or honey are safer for people with diabetes. The simple plan is to cut the total amount of sweet you eat, whatever its colour.

Why people switch to jaggery or honey

After a diabetes diagnosis, one of the first changes in many homes is: "no white sugar, use gud or honey." It feels like a healthy swap. Jaggery is less processed. Honey is natural. Both seem gentler than refined sugar.

But your blood does not care how a sugar was made. What matters is how much sugar reaches it and how fast. So the real question is whether these sweeteners behave differently in a body with diabetes. The best answer from the evidence is: not enough to matter.

What the WHO and ADA say

The World Health Organization uses the term free sugars. This means sugars added to food by the manufacturer, the cook or you, plus sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices and fruit juice concentrates. It does not include the sugars inside whole fresh fruit, vegetables or milk.1

The WHO advises keeping free sugars below 10% of your daily energy. Going below 5%, about 25 grams or 6 teaspoons a day, gives extra health benefit.1 That advice is for the general population. If you have diabetes, your doctor or dietitian may advise even less.

The American Diabetes Association advises people with diabetes to minimise sugar-sweetened drinks, sweets and refined foods, and to choose water instead of sugary drinks.2 It does not suggest that one type of sugar is safe to have more of.

Coconut sugar, date syrup, "organic" sugar, agaveMarketed as healthy. Syrups are also named by the WHO as free sugars.1 The same advice applies.
Be careful with "diabetic-friendly" claims. No sweet food is a medicine. Labels such as "sugar free", "no added sugar" or "made with gud" do not tell you how much carbohydrate or how many calories a product has. Read the nutrition label and check your own sugar after eating.

What about glycemic index (GI)?

GI is a score that ranks how fast a carbohydrate food raises blood sugar. Foods are usually grouped as low (55 or below), medium (56 to 69) and high (70 or above).3 You may see a "low GI" label on jaggery or honey. Be careful with such claims for three reasons:

  • The GI of honey varies a lot depending on its type, so one number does not describe all honey.
  • GI is measured with a fixed amount of carbohydrate. Real portions, and what you eat alongside, change the effect.
  • A lower GI does not mean you can eat more. Extra sugar still adds calories and still raises your blood sugar.

For a better way to judge a food, check your own sugar two hours after eating it. See our guide on tracking your numbers.

What to do instead

  1. Cut the total sweet, not just the type. If a recipe needs a teaspoon, try half. Taste adapts in a few weeks. See how to handle sugar cravings.
  2. Drop sweet drinks first. Cold drinks, packaged juices, sweet lassi and sherbet are the biggest sources of free sugar. Choose water. Our guide on chai, coffee and sweeteners covers tea and coffee.
  3. Count sweets as a treat, not a food. If you eat mithai or a spoon of honey, have it after a meal with fibre and protein, and take a walk. See post-meal walk.
  4. Eat whole fruit, not juice. The WHO's free-sugar definition leaves out the sugar in whole fresh fruit, but not in juice.1 Read our fruit guide.
  5. Plan ahead for festivals. See festival season with diabetes.
Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Is jaggery good for diabetes?

We did not find reliable human trials showing jaggery is safer than sugar for people with diabetes. It is an added sugar. Treat it like sugar and keep the amount small.

Is honey better than sugar for diabetics?

The WHO counts honey as a free sugar. Honey is not a treatment for diabetes. If you use it, count it as sugar and keep it small. Check your own sugar after eating it.

Which sweetener is safest if I have diabetes?

No sugar-based sweetener is safe to have freely. The ADA says non-nutritive sweeteners may replace sugary products if used in moderation and for a short period, and then the aim is to reduce your taste for sweet. Ask your doctor which option suits you.

How much sugar can I have in a day with diabetes?

The WHO advises the general population to keep free sugars below 10% of energy, and below 5% (about 25 grams, or 6 teaspoons) for extra benefit. With diabetes, ask your doctor or dietitian for your own limit.

Is fruit sugar the same as free sugar?

No. The WHO's free-sugar definition leaves out sugars inside whole fresh fruit, vegetables and milk. Fruit juice, however, counts as free sugar.

Can I eat mithai during festivals?

A small portion after a balanced meal, followed by a walk, is a more realistic plan than avoiding everything. Check your own sugar to see how you respond.
