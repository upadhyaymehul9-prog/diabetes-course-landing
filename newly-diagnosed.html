Just Diagnosed With Type 2 Diabetes? Your First Steps

You left with a diagnosis, maybe a prescription and a vague "watch your diet." Here is a calm starting point.

By Dr. Mehul Upadhyay · Last updated 27 November 2026
See the Course on Udemy →
12-week structured plan · Lifetime access · Certificate of completion
Short answer: do not panic, and do not wait. In your first weeks: (1) follow the plan your doctor gives you, (2) ask for diabetes education, which the American Diabetes Association advises for everyone with diabetes,1 (3) learn your HbA1c goal, which is below 7% for many adults,2 and (4) start small changes in food and movement. Earlier action gives better odds of remission.3

Your first week

A diagnosis is a shock. Most people feel fear, guilt or confusion. All three are normal, and none of them helps your sugar. What helps is a plan.

  1. Follow your doctor's plan. Take any medicine as prescribed, and do not stop it on your own.
  2. Ask about diabetes education. The American Diabetes Association advises everyone with diabetes to take part in diabetes self-management education and support.1
  3. Write down your numbers. Keep your HbA1c, weight and any home readings in one place. See tracking your numbers.
  4. Tell one person. Family support makes change easier. See for family members.

Questions to take to your next visit

  • What is my HbA1c, and what is my goal?
  • Which medicines am I on, and can any of them cause low sugar?
  • When is my next HbA1c test, and what else should be checked, such as kidneys, eyes and feet?
  • Can I see a dietitian or take a diabetes education class?
  • If I lose weight, how will we reduce my medicine safely?

Appointments are short. A written list helps you use them well. See also complications: what to watch.

Know your numbers

HbA1c shows your average sugar over about 2 to 3 months.2 Many non-pregnant adults are advised to aim for below 7%, though your own goal may be lower or higher.2 The ADA advises testing at least twice a year, and about every 3 months if you are not at goal or treatment has changed.2

New to these numbers? Read what your HbA1c means or use the free HbA1c calculator. Not sure you have diabetes yet? See early symptoms.

Safety first: low sugar

Low blood sugar (below 70 mg/dL) is the main danger of some diabetes medicines, such as insulin and sulfonylureas. Below 54 mg/dL is more serious, and a severe low needs another person's help.2

This matters most when you improve your diet or lose weight, because the same dose can become too strong. Do not skip meals, fast or cut medicine on your own. Read low blood sugar: signs and what to do.

What the ADA advises on eating

The American Diabetes Association advises that everyone with diabetes or prediabetes be referred to a registered dietitian for personal nutrition advice.1 Ask your doctor for that referral.

In general, its advice is to:1

  • Eat more non-starchy vegetables, whole fruits, legumes (dal, rajma, chana), lean proteins, whole grains, nuts and seeds.
  • Eat less sugary drinks, sweets, refined grains and ultra-processed foods.
  • Choose high-fibre carbohydrates, aiming for at least 14 g of fibre for every 1,000 calories.
  • Aim for at least 5 to 7% weight loss if you have overweight or obesity.

If you drink alcohol, the ADA advises staying within daily limits and knowing that sugar can drop hours later, especially on insulin or similar medicines. See alcohol and diabetes.

Start small

You do not need a perfect diet on day one. Pick two changes you can keep:

  • A 10 to 15 minute walk after meals. See the post-meal walk.
  • Fix your plate. See what to eat, an Indian diet plan and rice, roti or millets.

A simple first 90 days

This is our suggested pacing, not an official protocol. Adjust it with your doctor.

WhenFocus
Week 1See your doctor, take medicines as prescribed, ask for diabetes education and a dietitian, start writing down your numbers.
Weeks 2 to 4Add a 10 to 15 minute walk after your biggest meal. Fix one meal at a time, starting with the one you eat most often.
Weeks 5 to 8Extend walks, improve sleep, and check your readings with your doctor if they are changing. Watch for low-sugar signs if you are on insulin or sulfonylureas.
Weeks 9 to 12Repeat your HbA1c, which reflects about 2 to 3 months.2 Review the plan with your doctor and set the next goal.

If your first HbA1c is far above goal, your doctor may want to move faster. Follow their plan first.

Why acting early matters

In a 2025 study of 2,384 Indian adults, those with a shorter time since diagnosis, a lower starting HbA1c and no previous diabetes medicine were more likely to reach remission.3 The study had no control group, so it shows a link, not proof.

That is a reason to start now, not a promise. Read is reversal real? and the Indian study for the honest picture.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

What should I do first after a type 2 diabetes diagnosis?

Follow your doctor's plan, ask about diabetes education, learn your HbA1c goal, write down your numbers, and start small changes in food and walking. Do not stop or change medicine on your own.

What HbA1c should I aim for?

Many non-pregnant adults are advised to aim for below 7%, but your doctor may set a lower or higher goal depending on your health.

How often should I test my HbA1c?

At least twice a year. About every 3 months if you are not at goal or your treatment has changed.

Can type 2 diabetes go into remission?

For some people, yes. In studies it was more likely when people acted early and lost more weight. It is not a cure and not guaranteed.

Do I have to give up rice and sweets forever?

Not necessarily. Portions, timing and what you eat with them matter. See our guides on rice, roti and millets.

Should I tell my family?

Yes. Support at home makes lasting change easier. Share the family guide with them.
