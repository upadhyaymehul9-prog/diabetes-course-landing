Still Struggling With Type 2 Diabetes? Why It Is Hard and What Actually Helps

High numbers after real effort are not proof that you are lazy. Here is how to find what is missing.

By Dr. Mehul Upadhyay · Last updated 27 November 2026

Short answer: the American Diabetes Association says clinicians should take a non-judgmental approach that treats periodic lapses as normal.1 It also advises everyone with diabetes to get diabetes education and support,2 and to be checked for diabetes distress, depression and anxiety.3 If you are stuck, the missing piece is often support, not willpower.

Lapses are normal, not failure

The ADA's standards say that a non-judgmental approach that normalises periodic lapses in self-management helps people report problems honestly.1 They also say self-efficacy, which is your belief that you can manage your diabetes, is an important psychosocial factor linked to better outcomes.1

So the aim is not perfection. It is to keep returning to your plan quickly.

Ask for education and a dietitian

The ADA advises that everyone with diabetes take part in diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES), with culturally appropriate content, to help with decisions, self-care and problem-solving.2 It also advises referral to a dietitian.2

If you have only been told "eat less sugar", you may have missed the skills part. Start with newly diagnosed and an Indian diet chart.

Diabetes distress and low mood

An ADA position statement advises clinicians to consider assessing diabetes distress, depression, anxiety and disordered eating at the first visit, at regular intervals, and when life or health changes.3 Feeling worn down by diabetes is common, and it can get in the way of food, sleep and medicines.

Tell your doctor how you feel, not just your numbers. See stress, sleep and blood sugar and sugar cravings.

A one-week reset

This is our suggestion, not a tested protocol.

  1. Pick one habit. For example a 10-minute walk after dinner. See walking after meals.
  2. Measure for a week. Check fasting and 2-hour after-meal sugar. See fasting vs after-meal sugar.
  3. Find your worst meal and change one thing in it. See food order.
  4. Log it daily in our free tracker.
  5. Share the log with your doctor or dietitian.

When effort is not enough

Diabetes gets harder over time for many people, and some need medicine changes even with good habits. If your numbers stay high despite real effort, ask your doctor whether your treatment needs changing. Do not stop or change medicine on your own. See metformin and HbA1c explained.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Why is my blood sugar still high when I am trying?

Several things can be missing: skills, support, the right treatment, sleep, stress or low mood. Ask for diabetes education and a dietitian, and share your readings with your doctor.

Is it my fault if I slip?

No. The ADA advises a non-judgmental approach that treats periodic lapses as normal. The goal is to return to your plan quickly.

What is diabetes distress?

It is the emotional burden of living with diabetes. The ADA advises clinicians to assess for it, along with depression and anxiety.

Do I need diabetes education if I already know what to eat?

The ADA advises it for all people with diabetes, because it builds problem-solving and self-care skills, not just food knowledge.

Could I need medicine even if I eat well?

Yes. Diabetes often needs medicine changes over time. Talk to your doctor.

Where should I start this week?

Pick one habit, measure your sugar for a week, and share the log with your doctor or dietitian.
