Type 2 Diabetes After 60: Safer Blood Sugar Targets and What to Ask Your Doctor

After 60, the goal is not the lowest number. It is steady sugar, no lows, and staying independent.

By Dr. Mehul Upadhyay · Last updated 27 November 2026

Short answer: the American Diabetes Association says goals for older adults should be individual, based on health, thinking, daily function and other illnesses.1 For a healthy older adult its table suggests an HbA1c below about 7.0–7.5%. For someone with several illnesses or mild memory problems it suggests below 8.0%.2 Avoiding low sugar matters as much as lowering high sugar.

Your target depends on your health

The ADA advises doctors to look at four areas: medical, psychological, daily function (including your ability to manage diabetes yourself) and social support.1 Then they set goals.

Health statusReasonable HbA1c goal
Healthy, few other illnesses, thinking and daily function intactBelow 7.0–7.5%
Several illnesses, or two or more daily-activity limits, or mild to moderate memory problemsBelow 8.0%

This is from the ADA's 2024 table for clinicians.2 Your doctor decides which row fits you. Do not set a stricter target on your own. For what the number means, see HbA1c explained.

The ADA also advises testing HbA1c at least twice a year, and about every 3 months if you are off target or your treatment changed.3

Low sugar and falls

Older adults with diabetes have a greater risk of low blood sugar than younger adults. The ADA advises asking about low-sugar episodes at routine visits.4 The same guideline notes that older adults with diabetes are at higher risk of problems such as memory loss, depression and injurious falls.2

Know the signs and the fix. See low blood sugar: signs and the 15-15 rule. Keep a fast sugar source within reach, especially at night and when walking.

Medicines: ask, do not stop

The ADA's table includes ways to simplify a treatment plan, and when a doctor may reduce or stop a medicine (deintensification or deprescribing).2 That is a decision for your doctor, based on your health.

  • Bring all your medicines to every visit, including those from other doctors and any supplements.
  • Never stop or cut a medicine on your own. See metformin for one common example.
  • Ask if a simpler plan with fewer tablets is possible.

Food, movement and muscle

The ADA notes that older adults with diabetes have accelerated muscle loss.4 So for many people the aim is steady, healthy eating rather than crash dieting. Ask your doctor or dietitian before trying to lose weight.

Our suggestions: keep protein at each meal (dal, curd, eggs, fish), and add gentle strength work such as chair stands if your doctor agrees. See an Indian diet chart, what to eat and how much exercise.

Questions for your doctor

  1. Which HbA1c goal is right for me?
  2. Do any of my medicines raise my risk of low sugar?
  3. Can my plan be made simpler?
  4. Which complication checks do I need, and how often? See complications.
  5. How much weight loss, if any, is safe for me?

The ADA says screening for complications in older adults should be individualised and revisited from time to time.2

How family can help

Older adults with diabetes often need more help from caregivers.1 Family can help with pill boxes, meal planning, noticing low-sugar signs and going to appointments. See diabetes: a guide for families. Log readings together in our free tracker.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

What HbA1c is right for someone over 60?

It depends on health. The ADA's table suggests below 7.0 to 7.5% for healthy older adults and below 8.0% for those with several illnesses or mild memory problems. Your doctor sets your goal.

Is a lower HbA1c always better in old age?

No. Pushing too low can raise the risk of low blood sugar and falls. The goal is a safe, individual target.

Why is low blood sugar more dangerous for older adults?

Older adults are at greater risk of low sugar, and lows can lead to falls and confusion. Check episodes at each visit and keep fast sugar nearby.

Can an older adult try to reverse diabetes?

Some may be able to, but weight loss in older adults needs medical guidance because of muscle loss and medicine risks. Talk to your doctor first.

Can my doctor reduce my tablets?

Sometimes. The ADA describes simplifying or reducing treatment when appropriate. Never stop medicines on your own.

How often should HbA1c be checked?

At least twice a year, and about every 3 months if you are not at goal or your treatment changed.
