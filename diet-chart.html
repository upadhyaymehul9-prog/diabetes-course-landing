This isn't a diet brand and it's not a rigid meal plan you follow exactly every day. It's a structure you apply to almost any meal you already eat — including your own regional cuisine.

Does the order you eat in actually matter?

Yes. Eating your vegetables and protein first, and your rice or roti last, has generally been shown in meal-sequencing research to produce a smaller blood sugar spike than eating everything mixed together, or eating carbohydrates first. It's a small, free adjustment — no different quantity of food, just a different order. Try it yourself and watch what your own post-meal glucose readings show once you get to the tracking module.

Real Indian meal guidance, by region

India isn't one cuisine, so here's region-specific guidance rather than generic advice that doesn't match your actual kitchen. Roti, rice, and dal are not the enemy — what matters is portion and pairing, not elimination.

The structure
Roti, a vegetable sabzi, dal, often a side of salad or raita. Keep your roti portion moderate — ideally whole wheat or a millet blend. Make your sabzi genuinely vegetable-heavy, not swimming in oil with just a couple of pieces floating in it. Treat dal as a real portion, not an afterthought.
Watch for
Ghee and oil add up quickly in tadka, layered parathas, and rich gravies like paneer butter masala or dal makhani — often finished with a generous pour of cream or ghee. A small amount of fat is part of the plate model on purpose, but watch for fat layered on top of an already carb-heavy meal.
Practical swap: Shift from refined-flour (maida) roti to whole wheat first — usually the easiest transition, taking most people about a week or two to feel normal. Then experiment with a millet blend, starting at roughly one part bajra flour to three parts wheat atta.
The structure
Idli, dosa, sambar, and chutney are structured differently from North Indian meals. Idli, being steamed rather than fried, is a reasonably good option, especially paired generously with sambar — which contains dal and vegetables, giving built-in protein and fiber.
Watch for
A large, thin, crisp dosa is primarily refined-rice-batter — a fast carbohydrate, so portion matters more here than with idli. Pair dosa or idli with extra sambar and a vegetable-based chutney rather than coconut chutney alone.
Practical swap: If you make dosa batter at home, increase the lentil-to-rice ratio slightly — something like one part urad dal to two parts rice instead of a more rice-heavy ratio — to meaningfully improve the fiber and protein content.
The structure
Typically roti or bajra rotla, a vegetable sabzi, kadhi, and dal, often with a distinctive touch of sweetness characteristic of the cuisine. Gujarati cooking often includes jaggery or sugar in dishes you might not immediately expect — in kadhi, in certain dals, even in some sabzis like undhiyu.
What to do
None of this needs to disappear completely, and you don't need to cook food that no longer tastes like home. But being aware of where sugar hides matters, so you can moderate it deliberately rather than by accident.
Practical swap: When cooking at home, try moderating the added jaggery in kadhi and dal by roughly half of what you'd normally use, and genuinely taste the dish afterward. Most people's palates adjust within two to three weeks of consistent reduction.

Before you ask

Do I need to weigh my food to follow this?

No. You don't need to weigh or calculate anything — just notice the rough proportions on your plate and adjust gradually. Small, repeated adjustments beat one perfect plate you never repeat.

Do I need to give up roti, rice, and dal?

No. Nothing in this course asks you to give up the food you grew up eating. What actually matters is portion and pairing, not elimination — diet programs that ask people to eat food that feels foreign to them rarely get followed for twelve weeks, let alone for life.

My dal is usually thin and watery — does it still count as protein?

Dal is high in both fiber and protein, but a thin, watery dal treated as a flavoring liquid gives you less of both than it could. Consider making it slightly thicker, with more actual lentils per serving, and treat it as a genuine, substantial part of your meal.

Can I still eat white rice?

The fix isn't necessarily eliminating rice — for many households that's not realistic, and it doesn't need to be. The fix is portion and pairing: a smaller mound, roughly the size of your cupped palm, alongside a generous portion of dal and vegetable sabzi, produces a very different blood sugar response than a large plate of rice with a thin curry poured over it.

How much ghee or oil is actually allowed?

Roughly a teaspoon, used in cooking rather than piled on separately. Watch specifically for dishes where fat is layered on top of an already carbohydrate-heavy meal — rich gravies finished with cream or a generous pour of ghee add up quickly without adding much fullness.

Does this work for vegetarians?

Completely. The plate model needs protein, not meat specifically — dal, paneer, curd, tofu, chickpeas, and rajma all work. Swap the protein source, keep the same proportions.
