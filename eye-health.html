Plain English

What's actually happening in diabetic retinopathy

Over time, consistently high blood sugar can damage the small blood vessels in the retina — the light-sensitive layer at the back of your eye. In early stages, there are often no symptoms at all, which is exactly why screening matters more than waiting to notice a problem.

Early Stage

Mild, non-proliferative

Small changes in blood vessels, usually with no noticeable symptoms. Most commonly caught only through a dilated eye exam.

Progressing

Moderate to severe

More blood vessels affected; some people may start noticing blurred or fluctuating vision, though not always.

Advanced

Proliferative

New, fragile blood vessels can form; this stage carries the highest risk to vision and needs active medical management.

A Simplified Picture

Normal vision vs. advanced diabetic retinopathy

This is a simplified illustration of how the same everyday scene can look once retinopathy has reached an advanced stage — blurring, dark patches, and blind spots. Early stages usually show no visible change at all, which is why screening matters more than symptoms.

Normal Vision
Clear edges, full field of view, even color and contrast across the whole scene.
Advanced Retinopathy (Illustrative)
Blurred edges, dark patches or blind spots, and reduced overall field of vision — the specific pattern varies a lot person to person.

Illustrative only — not a diagnostic tool. Actual vision changes from retinopathy vary widely and should be assessed by an eye doctor.

Healthy Retina

Blood vessels are intact and evenly branching, with a clear, well-defined optic disc.

Retina With Retinopathy

Small hemorrhages (dark red dots) and abnormal, leaking vessels (dashed lines) — changes usually invisible to you, caught only through screening.

The Part That Actually Matters

Why "my vision feels fine" isn't a reliable test

What people assume

"If my eyesight feels normal, there's nothing to check yet."

What's actually true

Early-stage changes typically cause zero noticeable symptoms. A dilated eye exam can catch changes long before you'd ever feel anything different.

General guidance is a dilated eye exam at diagnosis and then at least once a year after — but your eye doctor will tell you what's right for your specific situation and risk level. This course doesn't replace that exam; it helps you understand why it's worth keeping on the calendar.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Can this course diagnose or check my eyes?

No — this is educational content only. A dilated eye exam by an eye doctor is the only way to actually screen for diabetic eye changes.

Is vision loss from this reversible?

It depends heavily on the stage and type of changes present — this is a conversation for your eye doctor, not something a general course can answer for your specific case.

How does the course relate to eye health?

Blood sugar management is one of the levers known to affect the progression of diabetic eye changes, and that's what the course's structured plan focuses on — alongside, not instead of, your regular eye exams.
