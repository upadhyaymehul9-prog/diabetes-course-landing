Faith and Blood Sugar

Fasting (Vrat) With Diabetes: Navratri, Ekadashi, Karva Chauth and Safer Ways to Do It

Millions of people with diabetes keep fasts. Most never talk to their doctor first. Here is what the research says, what can go wrong, and how to plan one safely.

Short answer: some people with type 2 diabetes can fast safely, and some should not. It depends on your medicines, your recent sugar readings and your other health problems. In a North Indian study, about half of people with type 2 diabetes had fasted in the past year, and only about 1 in 74 had discussed their diet with a doctor or dietitian before the fast. The most important step is the conversation you have before the fast, not the menu during it.

Why fasting needs a plan when you have diabetes

Fasting is part of daily life for many families. Navratri, Ekadashi, Karva Chauth, Mondays, Thursdays and many local traditions all involve changes to when and what you eat. For a person with diabetes, those changes can move blood sugar in both directions.

A questionnaire study of people with type 2 diabetes in North India found that about 49% had observed a fast in the last year. The most common were Navratri (about 35%) and Karva Chauth (about 31%). Only about 1.35% of participants had discussed their diet with a doctor or dietitian before observing the fast.1 The researchers concluded that poor dietary practices during fasts were common and that diabetes care teams should discuss fasting with their patients.1

A review of dietary advice for Navratri makes a similar point. It says a large number of people with diabetes keep the fast whatever the health effects, often without proper education or medical advice. It also notes that there is much less research on Hindu fasts than on Ramadan fasting.2

Two opposite dangers

  • Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia). Long gaps without food, while still taking insulin or certain tablets, can push sugar too low. Signs include shakiness, sweating, a pounding heart, confusion and sudden weakness. Read our page on low blood sugar and what to do.
  • High blood sugar (hyperglycaemia). Many vrat foods are heavy on starch, sugar or frying: sabudana khichdi, potato dishes, fried snacks, kheer and sweet drinks. Eating a large starchy meal after a long gap can cause a big rise.

There is a third risk: dehydration. If you also limit water, tea or other fluids, high sugar can get worse and you can feel dizzy and tired.

Never stop or change your diabetes medicines on your own for a fast. The doses and timing may need to change, and only your doctor can tell you how. See why in our page on not adjusting your own medication.

Who should be most careful, or should not fast

The best-developed guidance for fasting with diabetes comes from Ramadan. The International Diabetes Federation and the Diabetes and Ramadan (DAR) International Alliance published practical guidelines in 2021. They stress a medical risk assessment before the fast, patient education, glucose monitoring, and careful adjustment of medicines. They give special attention to pregnant women, older people and people with other chronic conditions.3

Ramadan fasting (no food or water from dawn to sunset) is different from most Hindu vrats, where some foods and drinks are allowed. But the safety principles carry over, and the Navratri review notes how much more guidance exists for Ramadan than for Hindu fasts.2 We apply those principles below. Please remember that we could not find trials of vrat diets for diabetes, so what follows is general clinical caution, not proven rules for each fast.

Talk to your doctor well before the fast if any of these apply to you:

  • You use insulin, or tablets that can cause low sugar (ask your doctor if yours do)
  • You have had low-sugar episodes recently, or your sugar swings widely
  • You have kidney disease, heart disease or other long-term conditions (see diabetes and your kidneys)
  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding
  • You are older or frail, or live alone
  • You were recently diagnosed and your sugar is not yet stable (see newly diagnosed)
  • You have type 1 diabetes

For some of these groups, the doctor may advise against a full fast. That is not a failure of faith. Many traditions allow a lighter form of observance for people who are unwell, and your priest or family elder can usually advise on what is acceptable. A lighter vrat, such as regular small meals of allowed foods, may keep the spirit of the day with far less risk.

A step-by-step plan before the fast

These are practical steps. They are not a substitute for your own doctor's advice.

  1. Book the talk two to four weeks ahead. Do not leave it to the evening before. Ask: Is it safe for me to fast? Which of my medicines need to change, and when do I take them on fast days? When should I break the fast?
  2. Check your sugar more often. Test at fixed times (for example morning, midday, evening) and any time you feel unwell. Write the numbers down. See how to track your numbers.
  3. Agree your break-the-fast rules. A reading below 70 mg/dL, or symptoms of a low, means treat it and eat. Do not "push through" for the sake of the fast. Your doctor will also give you an upper limit for very high readings.
  4. Keep fast-acting sugar within reach if you are at risk of a low, such as glucose tablets or a small juice.
  5. Stay hydrated during the allowed hours: water, buttermilk (chaas), or unsweetened lime water if your fast allows them.
  6. Break the fast gently. Start with a small portion, with protein or vegetables, rather than a large starchy or sweet meal. A big feast after a long fast is a common cause of high sugar.
  7. Do light movement. A short walk after your meal helps. Avoid hard exercise during long fasting hours. See walking after meals.

If you fast on many days, such as every Monday or Thursday, that is a regular pattern. It deserves a regular conversation with your doctor, not a one-time yes or no. Fasting for religious reasons is also not a structured treatment plan. Our page on intermittent fasting explains how that is different.

What to eat and avoid during a vrat

Many popular vrat foods are high in starch or fat. The aim is not to ban them, but to control the portion and balance the plate. These are general principles, not rules from a trial.

Often causes a big riseUsually a better choice (in sensible portions)
Large bowls of sabudana khichdi or vadaA small portion of sabudana, mixed with peanuts and vegetables, eaten after protein
Lots of potato, fried or boiledVegetables such as bottle gourd (lauki), pumpkin, cucumber and tomato
Deep-fried vrat snacks and chipsRoasted makhana, a small handful of nuts or roasted peanuts
Kheer, halwa, sweets, sweetened drinksCurd, buttermilk (chaas), paneer, if allowed in your vrat
Big portions of vrat flour rotis or purisA smaller portion of kuttu, singhara or rajgira flour roti with vegetables and curd
Fruit juice and sweetened milk drinksWhole fruit in a measured portion. See can you eat fruit with diabetes?

Fasting-day flours and grains are still carbohydrates. The way you plate them matters. Put vegetables and protein first and starch last, as we explain in does the order of eating matter? For festival days in general, see festival season eating.

Fried snacks are often made in repeated oil. They add calories and can hurt weight loss, which is a central goal in type 2 diabetes. If you can bake, roast or shallow-cook instead, do so.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Can a person with diabetes fast during Navratri?

Some can, and some should not. It depends on your medicines, your recent sugar readings and your other health problems. Speak to your doctor before the fast, especially if you use insulin or tablets that can cause low sugar.

How many people with diabetes fast, and do they talk to a doctor first?

In a North Indian study of people with type 2 diabetes, about 49% had observed a fast in the past year, with Navratri and Karva Chauth the most common. Only about 1.35% had discussed their diet with a doctor or dietitian before fasting.

Should I stop my diabetes medicine on fasting days?

Do not stop or change it on your own. Insulin and some tablets may need a dose or timing change when you fast, and some people may not be able to fast at all. Your doctor should tell you exactly what to do.

When should I break my fast?

Break the fast and treat a low if your blood sugar is below 70 mg/dL or you have symptoms such as shaking, sweating, confusion or sudden weakness. Ask your doctor for an upper limit too, because very high readings are also a reason to stop.

Is sabudana safe for diabetes?

Sabudana is mostly starch, so a large portion can raise sugar sharply. If you eat it, keep the portion small, mix it with peanuts and vegetables, and eat protein or vegetables first. Check your sugar two hours later.

Is religious fasting a good way to reverse diabetes?

We did not find evidence that religious fasting reverses diabetes. A vrat is a faith practice, not a structured treatment plan, and uncontrolled fasting can cause lows or highs. Our page on remission explains what is known.
