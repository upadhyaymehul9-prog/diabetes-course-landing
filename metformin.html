Metformin and Diabetes: Why You Should Never Adjust Your Medicine Yourself

As your numbers improve, your dose may need to change. That decision belongs to your doctor. Here is why, in plain words.

Short answer: metformin lowers sugar mainly by reducing how much sugar your liver releases, and it helps your body respond to insulin.1 On its own it does not usually cause low sugar.3 But insulin and sulfonylurea tablets can, and as you lose weight the same dose can become too strong.3,5 Never reduce or stop any diabetes medicine yourself. Ask your doctor to do it step by step.

How metformin works

Metformin lowers your blood sugar by improving the way your body responds to insulin, and by reducing the amount of glucose your liver releases into the blood.1 Doctors describe the liver effect as its main action.3

Because metformin does not push your pancreas to release more insulin, low blood sugar does not usually happen when it is used alone.3 A Mayo Clinic review of the drug makes the same point: one of its advantages is that it does not cause significant low sugar.2

Which medicines can cause low sugar

Low sugar can happen if metformin is taken with insulin, with a sulfonylurea tablet, or with a large amount of alcohol.3 If you take any of these, you are the person this page matters most for.

Know the numbers. Sugar below 70 mg/dL is level 1 low sugar. Below 54 mg/dL is level 2. A severe low, level 3, is any low that leaves you needing another person's help.5 The ADA says even one level 2 or level 3 episode should trigger a review of your treatment plan.5

Read low blood sugar: signs and what to do before you change anything in your food routine.

Why your dose may need to change as you improve

The American Diabetes Association says modest weight loss improves blood sugar and reduces the need for glucose-lowering medicines, and larger weight loss can reduce HbA1c and fasting sugar a lot.4

That is good news, but it has a catch. If your medicine dose stays the same while your body needs less, sugar can fall too far. This is most likely with insulin and sulfonylureas.3 It is why a doctor should lower doses in steps, guided by your readings.

Doing it yourself can go wrong in two ways: you can cut too little and still risk lows, or stop too early and let sugar climb. Neither is a good test. Read managing without medicine and is reversal real?

Side effects worth knowing

  • Stomach upset. Metformin is started at a low dose and raised slowly over weeks so your body can adjust.1 Stomach side effects can happen.2
  • Low vitamin B12. It can lower B12 levels over time, so ask your doctor whether to check it.1
  • Lactic acidosis. Rare, but serious. It can happen when the body makes a lot of lactic acid or cannot clear it.2 Tell your doctor about any kidney, liver or heart illness.
  • Scans with contrast dye. Metformin is usually paused around dye tests, so tell the scan team that you take it.3

Report a skin rash, severe itching or abnormal liver test results to your doctor.1

What to do instead

  1. Track your readings. Fasting and after-meal readings over at least 4 weeks give your doctor the trend. See tracking your numbers.
  2. Book a review as your numbers improve, not months later. Ask: "Which of my medicines can cause lows if I lose weight?"
  3. Carry fast sugar if you take insulin or a sulfonylurea.
  4. Never stop on your own because you feel fine. Sugar can stay high without symptoms.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

How does metformin lower blood sugar?

It reduces the amount of glucose your liver releases and helps your body respond better to insulin.

Does metformin cause low blood sugar?

Not usually when used alone, because it does not push the body to release insulin. Low sugar can happen if it is combined with insulin, a sulfonylurea or a lot of alcohol.

Can I stop metformin if my sugar is normal?

Not on your own. Sugar can rise again without symptoms. Ask your doctor, who may lower or stop it based on your readings.

Why might my dose need to change if I lose weight?

Weight loss reduces the need for glucose-lowering medicine. A dose that was right before can become too strong, mainly with insulin and sulfonylureas.

What are the common side effects?

Stomach upset can happen, mostly early on. Over time it can lower vitamin B12. A rare but serious problem is lactic acidosis. Report any concern to your doctor.

Should I stop metformin before a scan with dye?

It is usually paused around contrast dye tests, but follow your doctor's or the scan team's instructions.
