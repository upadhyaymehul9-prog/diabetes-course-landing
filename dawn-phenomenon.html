The Explanation

Why mornings are a different conversation

In the early morning hours, your body naturally releases hormones that signal your liver to release stored glucose — preparing you to wake up and function. In Type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance can mean this released glucose isn't cleared as efficiently, so the fasting number reads higher.

It's a hormone pattern, not a food mistake
This happens whether or not you ate anything the night before — it's driven by your body's own overnight hormone release, not last night's dinner.
It's common, not rare
A meaningful number of people with Type 2 diabetes see this pattern — you're not an outlier, and it isn't a sign your plan has failed.
It responds to structure, over time
Consistent sleep timing, evening meal composition, and — for some people — medication timing can influence how pronounced this pattern is.
What's Happening Overnight

A rough overnight timeline

Before Bed

Blood sugar may be in a reasonable range at lights-out.

Midnight – 3am

Levels are often stable or lower during the deepest part of sleep.

3am – 6am

Hormone release (cortisol, growth hormone) signals the liver to release glucose, readying your body to wake.

On Waking

Fasting reading reflects that release — this is the number you test.

Before You Ask

Frequently asked questions

Is this the same as "Somogyi effect"?

They're related but different mechanisms — one is a pure early-morning hormone release (dawn phenomenon), the other involves a rebound after an overnight low. Your doctor can help identify which applies to you, often by checking a 3am reading.

Should I change my medication because of this?

Never adjust medication based on this alone — discuss the pattern with your doctor, who may look at timing or dosage based on your full picture.

Does this mean the course "isn't working" for me?

No — this is a separate overnight hormone pattern, not a reflection of your daytime progress. The course's tracking module helps you tell the two apart.
